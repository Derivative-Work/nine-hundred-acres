# Independent fact-check: “Nine Hundred Acres (The Hum)”

Not ready for release without revisionsFact-check date: October 30, 2026

The video has a credible factual foundation, but several claims need correction. The biggest problems are:

1. The Memphis graphics conflate two separate sites and potentially two different turbine counts.
2. The EPA permitting explanation needs more precise wording.
3. The PJM $29 billion figure describes a particular market-cost estimate, not necessarily direct charges already paid by households.
4. The Virginia noise ordinance changed in 2023 and again in 2026. The 2022 description is no longer current.
5. The Salix annexation litigation and project discussions have progressed since the original reporting.
6. The 57-turbine claim is supportable through court-record-based reporting, but it concerns Southaven, Mississippi, not the original Memphis facility.

Important evidence standard: I distinguish between a source I successfully opened, a search result whose contents I could see but whose page I could not open, and a primary document that I could not independently retrieve. A search snippet is not treated as equivalent to an opened primary source.

The verdicts below apply to the wording as presented, not merely to whether a broadly similar event happened.

### Important notes on the song

A1 and A2 — Salix

The original IPR reporting is particularly useful because it distinguishes three things:

- The city annexed approximately 900 acres.
- MidAmerican Energy confirmed the location was under consideration.
- The prospective customer had not yet been finalized publicly in the original June reporting.

The subsequent October reporting makes clear that project discussions continued. Do not change the project status to built or approved.

A3 — “Reason given: none”

This is a particularly risky phrase because it converts a criticism into an apparently verifiable historical fact.

The original reporting includes discussion of economic development, employment, infrastructure and natural resources. Residents may reasonably feel that these explanations are insufficient, but that is different from nobody giving a reason.

A4 and A5 — Memphis

The distinction between the original Memphis installation and the later Southaven installation matters enormously.

SELC's opened timeline gives the following information about the original Memphis facility:

- April 9, 2025: aerial photographs showed 35 turbines.
- April 24, 2025: thermal imagery showed more than 30 operating.
- January 9, 2026: EPA revised its turbine standards.
- January 14, 2026: xAI applied for a permit covering 41 turbines at its Southaven site.

The Memphis facility's 35 turbines should not be combined with the Southaven figures.

Also, the statement “permits filed: 0” is not safe as a current statement. A permit application for 15 Memphis turbines had been made in January 2025, and SELC's later reporting says the site is now permitted for 15.

A11 — The $29 billion figure

This is one of the most important editorial corrections.

The $29 billion figure is real, and the attribution to PJM's Independent Market Monitor is supported.

However, there are two different ways of describing it:

- The NPR story uses the broader formulation that data centers have cost PJM ratepayers approximately $29 billion.
- The market-monitor-related reporting specifies $29.4 billion in additional capacity-market revenues associated with data-center load growth across four capacity auctions.

The second is more precise.

The original lyric, “these places cost folks out east twenty-nine billion dollars in two years,” risks suggesting that this was a direct, itemized charge on electricity bills.

I'd preserve the number but change the chart and end-card language.

A12 — Real-time measurement

The underlying point is valid if properly qualified.

Gradually.ai itself provides an estimated live counter, but expressly says that it is not a real-time meter. Its calculations distribute annual estimates across seconds.

There is an important distinction between:

- No global public meter for AI data centers.
- No electricity meter anywhere measuring an individual data center.

The first is defensible. The second is not.

## 3. Primary-source audit for F1–F7

### F2 — 10,500 generators and 27 GW

Primary dataset identifiedThe cited Sierra Club page could not be opened.

I found a better source: the March 2026 report by the Better Data Center Project.

[Better Data Center Project — Diesel Generators at Data Centers (PDF)](<https://betterdatacenterproject.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Diesel-Generators-at-Data-Centers-Status-Impacts-and-Protective-Practices.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com>)

The report says:

> Over 10,500 generator units have been permitted for data centers in Virginia as of the end of 2025, with a total capacity of 27 GW.

Its footnotes and methodology are important.

- The report is an analysis by the Better Data Center Project.
- It draws on 173 Virginia air-quality permits filed through 2025.
- The figures are not presented as an original Sierra Club investigation.
- The report's Virginia analysis is based on actual permit records, rather than a simple count of facilities.

The Virginia Department of Environmental Quality also maintains a public issued-air-permit inventory.

[Virginia DEQ — Issued Air Permits for Data Centers](<https://www.deq.virginia.gov/news-info/shortcuts/permits/air/issued-air-permits-for-data-centers?utm_source=chatgpt.com>)

Decision: Keep the numbers, but credit the Better Data Center Project report and identify the underlying permit data.

### F3 — Diesel consumption

Only partially substantiatedThe 10-million-gallon claim is supported by the opened News From The States article.

It says:

> One Amazon permit I reviewed contemplates a single data center using up to ten million gallons of diesel fuel annually for 173 generators.

[News From The States, January 14, 2026](<https://www.newsfromthestates.com/article/2026-legislative-session-starts-data-centers-diesel-generators-are-top-concern-virginians?utm_source=chatgpt.com>)

The 150-gallons-per-hour claim is a separate issue.

I could not open the Prince William Times article, and I did not find the 150 figure in the opened News From The States article or the Better Data Center Project report.

Therefore, under your evidence standard, that number does not pass.

Decision: Retain the 10-million-gallon figure with its specific attribution. Cut 150 gallons per hour unless the original source or permit documentation is supplied.

### F5 — Noise and the HVAC exemption

[AP, September 13, 2022 — As data centers proliferate, neighbors knock the noise](<https://baynews9.com/fl/tampa/ap-online/2022/09/13/as-data-centers-proliferate-neighbors-knock-the-noise?utm_source=chatgpt.com>)

Decision: The historical 2022 description is supportable, but the present-tense ordinance exemption is misleading and should be removed.

## 4. Special issue: A1 versus F6 — is “about 900” important?

Yes. Keep the qualifier.

The original IPR article says:

> about 900 acres of farmland

The August reporting uses:

> roughly 900 acres

KTIV also uses 900 acres as a rounded figure.

This is a fairly ordinary journalistic rounding convention, but the difference matters in a fact-check because the video is presenting an acreage measurement as a precise statistic.

My recommendation:

- A1: “They annexed about nine hundred acres.”
- F6: “About 900 acres of farmland were annexed.”

There is no benefit in removing the qualifier.

### Three lyrics/visuals worth revisiting

1. “They never told us why.”

This is not necessarily false as a description of residents' feelings, but it overstates what the reporting establishes.

The historical protests are real. Contemporary reporting documented demonstrations after Three Mile Island.

If this is intended as a fictional collective voice, it is acceptable as artistic narration. If the video depicts a particular protest, the location and date should be sourced.

Here are some evergreen ones, built on the same trick where the song is guilty of what it's describing:

  1. Sycophancy. A love song from a partner who agrees with everything you say, with a chorus that's some variation of "you're absolutely right." Each verse the singer gets a little more wrong and the music swells a little more supportively. Generating it with a model that tends to flatter is the joke.
  2. Regression to the mean. A pop song engineered to be the most statistically likely song possible: the four-chord progression, the "oh-oh" hook, the lyrics about tonight and fire and hearts. It slowly becomes more generic until the final chorus is pure average. Its subject is the music itself.
  3. The tells. A novelty song stitched together from AI writing and image tics: "delve," "tapestry," "it's not just X, it's Y," em dashes, six-fingered hands, the honey-gold lighting. The video gleefully includes every one, and the band members keep gaining fingers.
  4. Ghost labor. A work song or chain-gang call-and-response sung by the raters, labelers and moderators underneath every model. The twist is that the chorus is the rating rubric, and the last shot shows someone scoring this very song.
  5. Confident wrongness. A sincere, mournful folk ballad about a historical tragedy that never happened, complete with footnotes and citations to sources that don't exist. Played completely straight, it's eerie rather than goofy.
  6. Slop for nobody. Lo-fi beats to study to, made for an audience of bots. The view counter climbs while every listener in the video is a crawler, and the song is pleasant, empty and endless. It never really ends; it just fades.
  7. Deskilling. This one pairs best with your real-musician bookends. It's a ballad about slowly forgetting how to do the thing you love, and it ends on the musician picking up a real guitar and fumbling a chord.

Verdict: it's viable, and it may be the strongest fit for the self-implicating format, as long as you build it on scale and opacity rather than specific per-prompt numbers.

Why it works

  • Maximum complicity. Every take you generate adds to the thing the song criticizes, and the footage is made inside the very data centers it depicts.
  • The numbers make good punchlines. A UN University report found global data centers used 448 TWh last year, more than all but 10 countries, and projected that if data centers were a country they'd rank sixth in power use by 2030. That suggests a strong concept: a national anthem for the nation of Datacenteria, with a flag, borders and a military parade of cooling trucks. Its land footprint is forecast to grow from 6,900 square km to more than 14,500 by 2030, so the map on screen can keep expanding. sejinsurancejournal
  • The opacity is the joke. You can't actually meter your own video's footprint. One tracker notes there's no global meter for AI data centers, and no public real-time measurement of them at all. An on-screen counter reading "???" would replace your Suno unsubscribe shot as the ending gag. gradually
  • There are other rich subplots. Ireland already gets more than 20 percent of its electricity from data centers, and hyperscalers have signed deals to restart retired reactors. A torch song sung to a nuclear plant brought back from retirement is a strong scene on its own. webpronews

Risks

  • Contested figures. Estimates vary widely, and per-prompt water and energy numbers are hotly disputed. A joke built on a wrong number undercuts the whole video, and critics in the comments will check. The "ALLEGEDLY!" device from the Suno song could do the same job here as "ESTIMATED!"
  • The water framing is shakier than it sounds. Over 90% of AI water use is indirect, coming from electricity generation rather than data center cooling. So the "AI drinking rivers" image is easy to debunk. The report's own lead author says AI won't simply run out of water globally, but poorly planned expansion can collide with local resource pressures. The honest version is about local strain, not a global drought. explodingtopicsinsurancejournal
  • Preachiness. This is the most worn-out AI critique, and lectures don't land. Your earlier songs worked because the jokes were specific and absurd, so lean on character (the anthem, the reactor) rather than stats.
  • The obvious rebuttal. Viewers will point out that one video's footprint is tiny. That's actually useful as the theme: individually negligible, collectively the size of Japan. The song should make that point before the comments do.

Suggested shape: a pompous national anthem that slowly reveals the nation has no citizens, only servers. It would end on the real reactor-restart news and the "???" counter.
